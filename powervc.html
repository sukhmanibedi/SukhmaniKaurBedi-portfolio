IBM PowerVC

IBM PowerVC is built on OpenStack. It provides simplified virtualization management and cloud deployments for IBM AIX®, IBM i and Linux virtual machines (VMs) running on IBM® Power®. The offering is designed to build private cloud capabilities on Power servers and improve administrator productivity. It can further integrate with cloud environments through higher-level cloud orchestrators.

Focus Group System Admins
Sector Cloud Management
Role UX Researcher & UX Designer
Duration 23 months

Project overview

System administrators and technical users managing virtual machines (VMs) on IBM Power Systems via PowerVC face significant usability challenges due to a complex and cluttered user interface. The existing UI suffers from deep navigation hierarchies, visual clutter, and inadequate contextual guidance, leading to inefficiencies in performing critical tasks such as VM provisioning, patch management, and system scaling. These issues result in increased cognitive load, slower task completion times, and a suboptimal user experience, particularly when managing large-scale environments with thousands of VMs and volumes.

The project aims to redesign the PowerVC interface to provide a secure, scalable, and streamlined user experience that aligns with the workflows of system architects, administrators, and technical users. This involves conducting comprehensive user research, including generative and qualitative methods, to identify pain points and user needs. The goal is to enhance information architecture, reduce visual clutter, improve performance feedback, and incorporate contextual help, thereby enabling users to perform tasks more efficiently and with greater confidence.

Problem statement

PowerVC users were struggling with:

  • Complex, fragmented workflows for VM creation and management that involved multiple manual steps.
  • Inconsistent user experience between the GUI and API interfaces, causing confusion and errors.
  • Limited bulk operation capabilities, making management of numerous VMs tedious and time-consuming.
  • Lack of real-time feedback and error transparency during critical operations, leaving users uncertain about task status.
  • High training costs and skill barriers for new users due to interface complexity and inconsistent interactions.

The primary challenge was to streamline VM lifecycle management across both GUI and APIs, enhancing usability while maintaining the platform’s powerful capabilities.

Deriving the Three Hills in the PowerVC Project

At IBM, we follow the Enterprise Design Thinking (EDT) framework to align design, development, and business outcomes. A cornerstone of this approach is the Hill Statement — a clear, inspirational, user-centered goal that expresses what a user will be able to do, not just what the system will do.

In the PowerVC project, the Hill development process was research-driven, collaborative, and iterative, ensuring that our Hills represented real user needs while driving technical feasibility and innovation.

Step 1: Grounding in User Research

We began with an extensive research phase that included:

  • Generative research with 6–8 enterprise customers over a 6-month period
  • Qualitative analysis through PowerVC user forums
  • Heuristic evaluation of the existing platform
  • TechU survey to gather broad-based feedback on performance, usability, and needs

This effort highlighted key themes:

  • Users were concerned about security and reliability of updates.
  • System admins needed to handle large-scale environments without performance degradation.
  • Technical users wanted faster, frictionless experiences for common tasks.

Step 2: Persona Development & Role-Based Needs

We defined 3 core user personas, based on real roles and responsibilities:

  • Alan – System Architect: Focused on secure, stable infrastructure and patching without disruption.
  • Monica – System Administrator: Manages thousands of VMs and volumes daily; performance and scalability are critical.
  • Steve – Technical User: Executes routine VM tasks; values speed, ease, and reduced cognitive load.

These personas helped contextualize pain points and guided us toward crafting goals that are both aspirational and rooted in reality.

Using IBM’s Hill-writing practices, we conducted Hill Workshops involving UX designers, offering managers, engineering leads, design researchers, and stakeholder sponsors. Together, we transformed key user needs into Hill Statements — concise, powerful expressions of what success looks like for our users. Each Hill was framed around three criteria: Who is the user? What action or outcome will they achieve? Why is it valuable?

The Three Final Hills

“Alan, a system architect, can ensure his PowerVC UI is secure by applying updates on the fly.”
This Hill was derived from Alan's need for real-time patching and system integrity without causing downtime or disruption. The team prioritized a design that supports seamless updates with minimal risk.

“Monica, a system admin, can manage 10K VMs and 25K volumes using PowerVC, without any significant performance lag.”
Research showed Monica frequently works with high VM volume, and existing performance bottlenecks slowed her work. This Hill guided backend optimization and interface efficiency improvements.

“Steve, a technical user, can request a new VM in less than 1 minute.”
Steve needed to execute high-frequency tasks quickly. Research highlighted frustration with multi-step processes and delays, leading to a focus on simplifying and speeding up routine workflows.

Conducting Demos & Hosting Beta Trials

Conducting demonstrations serves as a pivotal step in ensuring design validation and a seamless user journey. These demos provide a platform to meticulously navigate users through the entire experience, validating the efficacy of our designs. By guiding users step by step, we gain valuable insights into usability, identifying potential hurdles, and refining the design to enhance user satisfaction. This iterative process of demo-based validation enables us to fine-tune each aspect, ensuring a user-centric journey that meets expectations and fosters a sense of ease and accomplishment.

We empowered users by granting them the capability to download the PowerVC test environment, allowing them to conduct testing on predefined scenarios. This hands-on approach enabled users to provide valuable feedback based on their experiences.

Additionally, we facilitated hosted beta testing, engaging in one-hour remote sessions with customers. During these sessions, users collaboratively reviewed the environment with our research and design team, fostering a dynamic exchange of insights. Subsequent to these trials, we distributed a survey to gather comprehensive feedback, further enriching our understanding and refining the product based on user input.

Key insights

Based on the initial data gathered there were areas that required to be addressed:

  • Consistency and standards: Consistency to visual and interaction conventions allows for better orientation and coherent behavior patterns. This also applies to consistency in language.
  • Flexibility and efficiency of use: With increased use comes the demand for less interactions that allow faster navigation. Users should be able to customize or tailor the interface to suit their needs so that frequent actions can be achieved through more convenient means.
  • Aesthetic and minimalist design: Aesthetic considerations should be kept simple and avoid non-useful clutter. Function should always come ahead of form.
  • Include wizards to help complete major tasks: Provide handholding for major tasks that have multiple steps required to ensure the entire process is followed.
  • Improve quality of information: When help and instructions are needed, they should be accessible, understandable and accurate. This could be represented in the form of a search bar, links to resources, tool tips, etc.
  • Recognition rather than recall: Minimize cognitive load by maintaining task-relevant information within the display while users explore the interface. Give users options/suggestions to choose from, instead of having to think up possible options themselves.
  • Improve navigation: Information architecture is very deep and it is difficult to find certain features.

The discovery

We pinpointed primarily minor issues, which can be readily resolved without encountering any critical problems. However, these seemingly minor issues, from a user experience standpoint, do hinder a seamless and intuitive journey. General sentiment from NPS is that PowerVC is a great product that simplifies deployments and management and offers the possibility of a private cloud, but it can be quirky.

  1. The information architecture, which extended quite deeply, reaching up to 6 levels. Users were occasionally able to link to other panels in ways that lacked coherence, a sentiment confirmed through customer research sessions.
  2. There is no flow in completing a task, a lot of context shifts are there.
  3. The system's performance occasionally lags, while certain feedback messages prove perplexing or irrelevant.
  4. The pace of interaction suffers delays due to misleading cues, technical intricacies, and inadequate feedback messages.
  5. An absence of upfront contextual information or help demands extra time and effort from users.
  6. Panels tend to be overcrowded, hosting multiple focal points that contribute to an overall sense of chaos and disharmony.

OOBE Workshop: Redesigning the PowerVC Onboarding Experience

Following extensive user research on the Out-of-Box Experience (OOBE), we conducted a focused internal workshop to translate insights into actionable design improvements.

🎯 Objective: To streamline the onboarding journey for PowerVC by addressing user pain points, clarifying setup flows, and identifying quick wins.

🧠 Process: We kicked off with a mind mapping session, clustering user feedback into key themes like installation complexity, unclear prerequisites, and first-time setup confusion. Using dot voting, we prioritized issues based on impact and frequency.

Next, we grouped findings under key topics: Environment Planning, Installation Flow, GUI Setup, First VM Creation.

Each team member then individually brainstormed and mapped a revised onboarding flow, tailored to their assigned topic. We reconvened to present and evaluate ideas, focusing on feasibility, user impact, and alignment with technical constraints.

✅ Outcome: The workshop led to a draft of an improved, role-based OOBE, several feasible design enhancements, and clear next steps for prototyping. It also reinforced cross-functional alignment between design, product, and development team.

Design validation & Complexity Analysis

Conducting design demonstrations and performing Complexity Analysis work hand‑in‑hand to ensure every release of PowerVC delivers an intuitive, friction‑free experience.

Demo‑driven validation

  • Live walk‑throughs let us observe users moving end‑to‑end through new flows, capture real‑time feedback, and spot hidden friction.
  • Step‑by‑step guidance during the session surfaces language gaps, confusing transitions, or missing cues we might overlook in static reviews.
  • Insights gathered feed directly into rapid iterations, tightening the loop between research and design and guaranteeing the UI stays grounded in actual user behaviour.

Complexity Analysis

  • Borrowed from IBM’s internal research toolbox, Complexity Analysis quantifies how difficult each step of a task feels to users.
  • Participants rate steps on six dimensions—context shifts, input parameters, navigational guidance, system feedback, error feedback, and new concepts.
  • The resulting “complexity map” highlights high‑load moments, helping teams prioritise simplification efforts where they matter most.

Unified impact

By pairing demonstration sessions (qualitative why & how) with Complexity Analysis scores (quantitative where & how much), we:

  • Validate that new designs solve the right problems before code is hardened.
  • Pinpoint exact steps that still feel heavy or confusing, even if they “work.”
  • Measure improvements release over release, tying UX changes to lower complexity scores and faster task‑completion times.

The result is a rigorously tested, user‑centred onboarding journey that feels streamlined on day one—and gets measurably simpler every iteration.
